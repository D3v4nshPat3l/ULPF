One attacker. Four logs. Four different languages.

Someone is given a suspicious IP address and asked: where has this been on our network? The address is already sitting in four different logs. But each product writes it in a different spot, in its own style — so before asking a single question, the person has to know how all four products write things down. The six things, in the order they matter

These follow a real investigation from start to finish: everything arrives → you search it → you go back and look at the original → you check nobody has quietly edited it → you prove it to someone else → and that proof still has to work a year later, in court.

01

Everything gets in. Nothing gets thrown away.

We mixed eight real products' logs into one file — Snort, Linux firewall, Squid, Apache, Dragon, Zeek, Linux system log and the mail server — jumbled together the way they would actually arrive. Then we handed the whole mess to ULPF without telling it what was in there.

28 lines were in a format it had never seen. It kept all 28 anyway, with their contents intact, so 10 of them can still be found by searching for an IP or a username. This matters: most tools silently bin what they cannot read, and the one line you needed is usually the strange one.

02

One search instead of four

Seven of the eight products record who the traffic came from. After ULPF, all seven put it under the same name. So the question "where else has this address been?" is now one search that covers the whole network — instead of four searches, in four styles, that only cover the products you happen to know.

This is the single biggest time saving on the page, and it happens before an investigation even begins.

03

The original line is always still there

This is where most tools lose the evidence. They tidy a log line into neat fields, keep the fields, and throw the original line away. If a lawyer later asks "what did the machine actually print?", there is no answer.

ULPF keeps every original line and stores a fingerprint of it — a short code that changes completely if even one character changes. Ask for the original back, re-take its fingerprint, and it matches the one recorded when the line first arrived. That is what proves it is untouched.

04

Change one character and it shows

An attacker's last move is usually to edit the logs and delete themselves from them. Every line here is fingerprinted, and each fingerprint is locked to the one before it — like a sealed chain. Break one link and the whole chain reports it.

We tested it: out of 20,338 lines we changed one address on one line, exactly the edit an intruder would make, and ran the check again. It did not shrug or warn — it failed outright, and reported failure in a way a machine can act on, so this can run automatically every night instead of relying on someone reading a screen.

05

Prove one line without showing the other 49,999

Say you need to prove in court that one specific line was in your logs. Normally you must hand over the entire log file — which means disclosing everything else in it too: other customers, other staff, unrelated incidents.

Instead ULPF issues a receipt for that one line. It is 2,261 bytes — about the size of a text message — and it is enough to prove that line was present, without revealing a single other line. The other side can check it on their own computer, offline, with nothing from us. This uses the same method that secures every HTTPS certificate on the internet.

We then tried to cheat it four different ways, including forging the receipt and editing the line it refers to. All four were caught, and each time it said exactly what was wrong.

06

Today's receipt still works next year

Court cases take years, and logs keep growing the whole time. A receipt issued today describes the log as it looked today — so by the hearing, it could easily be worthless.

We tested this by collecting another 10,000 events and re-checking the old receipt. It still works. A 416-byte note proves the old log is simply the beginning of today's log, unchanged — nothing was rewritten in between. Evidence handed over years ago never needs to be reissued, and cannot quietly be invalidated by us continuing to collect.

What we are not claiming

These are the numbers that would have made this page look better, and that we have not earned yet. They are printed here on purpose: you should be able to tell which of our figures were measured and which were only calculated.

864 million a day
That is multiplication, not a measurement. It is 10,000 × 86,400 seconds in a day — stretched from a ten-second test out to a full day, a jump of 8,640×. We have not run it for a day. The speed we have measured is 20,179 lines a second across eight mixed products.
1 billion a day
Not achieved yet. It needs several machines running together for a full day, which we have not done. It is our goal, and we call it a goal — see the next line for the problem currently in the way.
Our largest test file
It did not finish, and we still do not know why. An 8,130,590-line proxy log ran past a 30-minute limit. We spent this session trying to explain it and can report honestly that we cannot yet. Our leading theory — that the tamper-proof seal gets slower as the log grows — was tested directly by feeding the same 200,000 lines five times into an ever-larger log, and it was disproved: the run against the largest log was the fastest of the five. Measured speed swings between 7,400 and 20,400 lines a second for reasons we have not isolated. Until we can explain it, this file's numbers stay out of every speed figure on this page, and this is the open problem between us and the billion-a-day goal.
“100% accurate”
100% on our own 81 test cases — not on the whole world. Those are 81 lines we checked by hand, covering the 37 products we support. Passing them means the tool really does what we said it does. It is not a promise about a product nobody has taught it yet.

The one thing to remember

ULPF does not catch attackers. It makes the evidence usable. It sits in front of the tools that do the catching, and changes what they have to work with: every product speaking one language, every original line still there and provably untouched, and any single line provable to someone who does not trust you — without showing them the rest.

Put simply: it removes the half hour of translation before an investigation can start, and it keeps the answer standing up in the room where it eventually gets questioned.

Every figure above came out of ulpf 0.1.0, commit f9287a7, run on 2026-09-10 on one laptop — nothing here is estimated or typed in by hand.
The logs are real public attack captures: the Honeynet Project (Scans of the Month 30 and 34) and the Loghub research collection — 345,070,506 genuine records, 59.63 GB. None of it is invented.
